## Notes and references

The chapter proves the [covolume bound of $X$](/step/covolume-bound-of-x-def/) in two forms: from seven groups of hypotheses in [the covolume bound](/entry/top-covol-g-alt-alt/), and from fifteen results of the literature in [the second theorem of that section](/entry/top-covol-slim2-use-alt-alt/). The second form is the one used in [the literature branch](/step/every-character-of-literature/) of the proof. Sections 1 to 9 carry the main argument: the language of level and cover statements and the [classical inputs](/entry/to-classical-theorems/), the separation of orbits by cusp forms, the flat sections at the last level, the low defect bound, the reference exponents, the Bergman kernel and the carrying section, the Hilbert-Samuel estimates, the height lower bound and the assembly. Sections 10 to 17 prove inputs that the main argument quotes: the equivariant linearizations used for the orbit separation, the log poles and the log canonical criterion behind the vanishing theorems, valuation multiplicity and birationality, the flat degree condition and the flat units, the regularity of good covers, the bad set and measure conditions of the lift, the equality of volumes, and the two volume estimate hypotheses.

The arithmetic part of the chapter is a form of the arithmetic Hilbert-Samuel theorem with a lower bound for heights of lattices of cusp forms. The arithmetic Hilbert-Samuel theorem is due to Gillet and Soulé, as a consequence of their arithmetic Riemann-Roch theorem, and the intersection theory used throughout is theirs ([arithmetic intersection numbers](/learn/arakelov/#arithmetic-varieties-and-intersection-numbers)). The slopes and covolumes of Sections 4 and 9 are those of [hermitian lattices](/learn/arakelov/#hermitian-lattices-and-slopes). In the classical treatment one fixes a projective arithmetic variety and a hermitian line bundle and lets the power grow; here the variety changes along a family of unitary Shimura data, and every estimate is a level or cover statement whose constant is uniform over the family. This uniformity is the reason for the bookkeeping of Section 1, and it has no classical counterpart that we know of.

Mumford proportionality, quoted in Section 1 and used in Sections 11 and 16, is Mumford's form of Hirzebruch proportionality for non-compact quotients, with the canonical extension of automorphic bundles to a toroidal compactification and the identification of the canonical weight bundle with the sheaf of top forms with log poles along the boundary ([Mumford's proportionality theorem](/learn/abelian-varieties/#mumfords-proportionality-theorem)). The equality of volumes in Section 16 is a corollary: the volume of a component is its covolume times a universal constant, and the components of a good level have the same covolume. The Lean form measures volume by the growth of the dimensions of spaces of cusp forms, so it also uses asymptotic Riemann-Roch.

The toroidal compactifications of Sections 2 and 11 to 13 are those of Faltings and Chai for the Siegel case and of Lan for PEL type, with the log Kodaira-Spencer isomorphism ([toroidal compactifications](/learn/geometry/#shimura-varieties-and-toroidal-compactifications), [the Kodaira-Spencer map](/learn/abelian-varieties/#the-kodaira-spencer-map)). The comparison in Section 11 of the log Kodaira-Spencer matrix with the Kodaira-Spencer map of the deformation theory is the local content of that isomorphism; the deformation theory is Grothendieck's, in the form of Messing. The Koecher principle of Section 14, that a bounded form of weight zero is constant on a component, is in Faltings and Chai, Chapter V.

Section 12 is the criterion that a modular form of weight $\kappa + b$ is a cusp form if and only if the associated top form extends holomorphically to a smooth compactification. For Siegel modular forms this is classical and is the starting point of the work on the Kodaira dimension of Siegel modular varieties; the toroidal form is a direct consequence of $L(\kappa) \cong \Omega^n(\log D)$. The Lean form proves it on the toroidal flag space and on every smooth projective modification, and treats the case $p + q = 0$ separately. The language of log canonical pairs follows Kollár and Mori.

The birationality theorem of Section 13 uses Nadel vanishing for multiplier ideals and the point separation theorem of Angehrn and Siu ([vanishing theorems and resolution](/learn/geometry/#vanishing-theorems-and-resolution), [point separation](/learn/geometry/#point-separation)). Classically one gets from Angehrn-Siu that adjoint bundles $K + L$ separate points once $L$ is ample with large enough intersection numbers; here the bound $(n + 3)^2 + 2(p + q)$ on the gaps of a weight plays that role, and the log canonical criterion of Section 12 turns cusp forms into sections of adjoint bundles. The valuation multiplicity theorem of the same section has no classical source that we know of; its two inputs are bounds for orders of vanishing at the boundary, measured by a slope invariant at integral points.

The equivariant linearizations of Section 10 are linearizations in the sense of geometric invariant theory, Mumford, Chapter 1, §3. The orbit separation of Section 2 uses that the Hodge line bundle is nef, by the seminegativity of the Lie algebra of a semi-abelian scheme (Moret-Bailly), and big.

The Chow form heights of Section 17 are the heights of Bost, Gillet and Soulé ([heights](/learn/arakelov/#heights)): for a projective arithmetic variety embedded by sections, with the Fubini-Study metric, the self-intersection of the line bundle is the height of the Chow form of the image. The Lean statement is an inequality, but its proof gives an equality. The bound on the number of small sections is the counting step of the arithmetic Hilbert-Samuel argument, in the form used for arithmetic volumes ([arithmetic volume and bigness](/learn/arakelov/#arithmetic-volume-and-bigness)).

The flat degree condition of Section 14 is proved by resolution of singularities and principalization of ideals in characteristic zero, which are due to Hironaka; constructive proofs are due to Bierstone and Milman and to Włodarczyk, and Kollár presents them in his lectures ([resolution of singularities](/learn/geometry/#resolution-of-singularities), [resolution and principalization](/learn/positivity-resolution/#resolution-of-singularities-and-principalization)). The degree of a flat section is computed as a period, $\frac{p - q}{2} \log \Omega$, as in [the section on flat sections](/entry/flat-exists-at-core-alt/).

**Remark 2.18.1 (Formalization).** The flat degree condition rests on `UpperTheory.Lit.Flat.flat_tensor_swap_isIso` ([FlatTensorSwapIsIso.lean](lean:Development/AutomorphicArithmetic/ThetaCorrespondence/ThetaPairLifts/HodgeBundleWeights/FlatTensorSwapIsIso.lean#L7)). Its statement `T.FlatDegreeHypotheses` asks for the published list, the rank one canonical extension and the prismatic Dieudonné theory with its Hodge filtration, and concludes the uniform flat degree statement `FlatDegreeSt`; the proof ignores all three hypotheses (`fun _ _ _ => ...`) and derives the conclusion from `flatDegreeSt_of_resK` with the resolution package `resK`, which is built from resolution of singularities in characteristic zero and principalization. Its name speaks of a tensor swap being an isomorphism, which does not describe the statement. The same package `resK` is used in the arithmetic bigness proof of another chapter. The theorem numbers of the sources above were not checked against the Lean statements; where a chapter is given, it is the chapter where the cited topic is treated.
