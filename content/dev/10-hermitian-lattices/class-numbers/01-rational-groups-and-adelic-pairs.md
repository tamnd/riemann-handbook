## Rational groups and adelic pairs

This chapter proves that a linear algebraic group over a number field has finite class number: the finite adelic points are covered by finitely many double cosets of the rational points and an open subgroup. The proof goes to $\mathbb{Q}$ by restriction of scalars, and then works with a group of matrices of determinant $1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and an abstract pair of rings $(R, R_0)$ that behaves like the finite adeles and their integral elements. This section sets up the three objects of the proof: the points of a set of complex matrices over a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra, rational groups, and adelic pairs. It ends with the first case of the main theorem, class number one for the special linear group, proved with lattices in $\mathbb{Q}^n$.

### Points over an algebra

Let $I$ be a finite set. For a commutative ring $A$ let $M_I(A)$ be the ring of matrices with rows and columns indexed by $I$ and entries in $A$ (see [matrices](/learn/algebra/#matrices)), and $\mathrm{SL}_I(A)$ the group of matrices of [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] $1$. Let $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ be the polynomial ring over $\mathbb{Q}$ in the $|I|^2$ variables $x_{ij}$, $i, j \in I$ (see [polynomials](/learn/algebra/#polynomials-and-power-series)). For a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra $A$, a polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ and $g \in M_I(A)$, we write $f(g) \in A$ for the value of $f$ at the entries of $g$.

**Definition 1.1.1 (Points of a set of matrices).** Let $\Gamma \subseteq M_I(\mathbb{C})$. The *rational ideal* of $\Gamma$ is
$$\mathfrak{a}(\Gamma) = \{ f \in \mathbb{Q}[x] : f(g) = 0 \text{ for all } g \in \Gamma \}.$$
For a commutative $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra $A$, the set of *$A$-points* of $\Gamma$ is
$$\Gamma(A) = \{ g \in M_I(A) : f(g) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in \mathfrak{a}(\Gamma) \}.$$

So $\Gamma(\mathbb{C})$ is the smallest set that contains $\Gamma$ and is cut out by polynomials with rational coefficients: it is the Zariski closure of $\Gamma$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. The sets $\Gamma(A)$ depend only on the ideal $\mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$. A rational matrix $\gamma \in M_I(\mathbb{Q})$ with $\gamma \in \Gamma$ lies in $\Gamma(\mathbb{Q})$, and the image in $M_I(A)$ of an element of $\Gamma(\mathbb{Q})$ lies in $\Gamma(A)$, since $f(\gamma)$ maps to $f$ evaluated at the image.

**Definition 1.1.2 (Galois-stable group).** A set $\Gamma \subseteq M_I(\mathbb{C})$ is a *Galois-stable group* if $1 \in \Gamma$, $gh \in \Gamma$ and $\operatorname{adj}(g) \in \Gamma$ for all $g, h \in \Gamma$, $\det g = 1$ for all $g \in \Gamma$, and $\sigma(g) \in \Gamma$ for every $g \in \Gamma$ and every field automorphism $\sigma$ of $\mathbb{C}$, applied to the entries.

Since $\det g = 1$, the adjugate $\operatorname{adj}(g)$ is the inverse $g^{-1}$. So a Galois-stable group is a subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}_I(\mathbb{C})$.

**Definition 1.1.3 (Rational group).** A Galois-stable group $\Gamma$ is a *rational group* if $\Gamma(\mathbb{C}) \subseteq \Gamma$.

Since always $\Gamma \subseteq \Gamma(\mathbb{C})$, a rational group is the set of complex points of the ideal $\mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$: it is the group of complex points of a linear algebraic group over $\mathbb{Q}$ inside $\mathrm{SL}_I$. The condition on automorphisms of $\mathbb{C}$ makes the rational ideal $\mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$ large enough to control $\Gamma$; it is used in each step below where a complex object must be shown to be rational.

**Example 1.1.4.** The group $\Gamma = \mathrm{SL}_I(\mathbb{C})$ is a rational group. Since $\det - 1$ is irreducible, $\mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$ is the ideal generated by $\det - 1$, and $\Gamma(A) = \mathrm{SL}_I(A)$ for every $A$. A finite subgroup $\Gamma \subseteq \mathrm{SL}_I(\mathbb{Q})$ is also a rational group, and then $\Gamma(\mathbb{Q}) = \Gamma$.

**Definition 1.1.5 (Completely reducible).** A set $\Gamma \subseteq M_I(\mathbb{C})$ is *completely reducible* if every subspace $W \subseteq \mathbb{C}^I$ with $gW \subseteq W$ for all $g \in \Gamma$ has a complement $W'$ with $gW' \subseteq W'$ for all $g \in \Gamma$.

For a group this says that the [[group representation|WYGRXCG2RN]] $\mathbb{C}^I$ is semisimple (see [representations of a group](/learn/representations/#representations-of-a-group)). A finite group is completely reducible by Maschke's theorem. The group of upper triangular unipotent $2 \times 2$ matrices is not: the line $\mathbb{C} e_1$ is stable and has no stable complement.

**Lemma 1.1.6 (Points form a group).** Let $\Gamma \subseteq M_I(\mathbb{C})$ be a Galois-stable group and $R$ a commutative $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra. Then $1 \in \Gamma(R)$, the set $\Gamma(R)$ is closed under products and adjugates, and $\det x = 1$ for every $x \in \Gamma(R)$.

Lean: `GlobalTheory.T.ratZariskiClosurePoints_isGroup` (also `GlobalTheory.ClassFin.pts_group`, a second name).

*Proof.* Let $f \in \mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$. Since $f(1) = 0$ in $\mathbb{C}$ and $f$ has rational coefficients, $f(1) = 0$ in $\mathbb{Q}$, so in $R$. The polynomial $f(\operatorname{adj}(x))$ lies in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and vanishes on $\Gamma$, since $\Gamma$ is closed under adjugates. So it lies in $\mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$, and it vanishes at each $x \in \Gamma(R)$. In the same way $\det - 1 \in \mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$, so $\det x = 1$ on $\Gamma(R)$. For products, let $F(x, y) = f(xy)$, a polynomial with rational coefficients in two matrices of variables. It vanishes on $\Gamma \times \Gamma$. We use the following fact: a rational polynomial in two matrices of variables that vanishes on $\Gamma \times \Gamma$ vanishes on $\Gamma(R) \times \Gamma(R)$, when $\Gamma$ is stable under $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C})$. So $f(xy) = 0$ for $x, y \in \Gamma(R)$. $\square$

### Adelic pairs

The finite adeles $\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}, f} = \widehat{\mathbb{Z}} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q}$ (see [adeles](/learn/number-fields/#adeles-and-ideles)) contain $\mathbb{Q}$ and the subring $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, with $\mathbb{Q} \cap \widehat{\mathbb{Z}} = \mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}, f}$, so $\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}, f} = \mathbb{Q} + M \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ for every integer $M \geq 1$. The proof below uses only these two properties.

**Definition 1.1.7 (Adelic pair).** Let $R$ be a commutative $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra and $R_0 \subseteq R$ a subring. The pair $(R, R_0)$ is an *adelic pair* if

1. every $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ whose image in $R$ lies in $R_0$ is an integer, and
2. for every integer $M \geq 1$ and every $r \in R$ there are $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $a \in R_0$ with $r = q + Ma$.

A matrix with entries in $R_0$ is called *integral*.

**Example 1.1.8.** The pair $(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}, f}, \widehat{\mathbb{Z}})$ is an adelic pair, by the two facts above. The pair $(\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{Z})$ is also an adelic pair: condition 2 holds with $q = r$ and $a = 0$. The two conditions are weak on purpose. The content of the theorems below is in the case where $R$ is large, as for the finite adeles.

**Lemma 1.1.9 (Elementary properties).** Let $(R, R_0)$ be an adelic pair. Then:

1. for every integer $M \geq 1$, every $r \in R_0$ is $r = z + Ma$ with $z \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $a \in R_0$;
2. for every finite family $(r_k)$ in $R$ there is an integer $M \geq 1$ with $M r_k \in R_0$ for all $k$;
3. the map $\mathbb{Q} \to R$ is injective.

Lean: `GlobalTheory.ClassFin.adelicPair_elementary`.

*Proof.* For 1, write $r = q + Ma$ with $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $a \in R_0$. Then $q = r - Ma$ lies in $R_0$, so $q \in \mathbb{Z}$ by condition 1. For 2, write $r_k = q_k + a_k$ with $M = 1$ in condition 2, and let $M$ be a common denominator of the $q_k$. Then $M r_k = M q_k + M a_k \in \mathbb{Z} + R_0 \subseteq R_0$. For 3, the kernel of $\mathbb{Q} \to R$ is an ideal of the field $\mathbb{Q}$. If it is $\mathbb{Q}$, then $R = 0$, so $1/2$ maps into $R_0$ and condition 1 fails. $\square$

So $R$ contains $\mathbb{Q}$, every element of $R$ has a denominator, and an element of $R_0$ is an integer modulo $M R_0$ for every $M$.

**Definition 1.1.10 (Class finiteness).** Let $\Gamma \subseteq M_I(\mathbb{C})$ and let $(R, R_0)$ be a pair of a commutative $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra and a subring. We say that *$\Gamma$ has finite class number over $(R, R_0)$* if there is a finite set $T \subseteq \Gamma(R)$ such that every $x \in \Gamma(R)$ is a product
$$x = \gamma \, s \, u, \qquad \gamma \in \Gamma(\mathbb{Q}), \quad s \in T, \quad u \in \Gamma(R) \cap M_I(R_0).$$

For a rational group, $\Gamma(R)$ is a group by the lemma above, and $\Gamma(R) \cap M_I(R_0)$ is closed under products and adjugates. The statement is that the double coset space $\Gamma(\mathbb{Q}) \backslash \Gamma(R) / \Gamma(R_0)$ is finite. The definition depends only on $\mathfrak{a}(\Gamma)$, since the sets $\Gamma(\mathbb{Q})$ and $\Gamma(R)$ do.

### Class number one for the special linear group

**Theorem 1.1.11 (Class number one for $\mathrm{SL}$).** Let $(R, R_0)$ be an adelic pair and $x \in M_I(R)$ with $\det x = 1$. Then there are $h \in M_I(\mathbb{Q})$ with $\det h = 1$ and an integral $k \in M_I(R_0)$ with $\det k = 1$ such that $x = hk$.

Lean: `GlobalTheory.T.specialLinear_classNumber_one_adelic` (also `GlobalTheory.ClassFin.sl_class_number_one`, a second name).

For $(R, R_0) = (\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}, f}, \widehat{\mathbb{Z}})$ this is $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}, f}) = \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Q}) \, \mathrm{SL}_n(\widehat{\mathbb{Z}})$, a case of [strong approximation](/learn/groups/#strong-approximation). The proof below uses lattices instead. It is the classical proof that every lattice in $\mathbb{Q}^n$ has a basis.

*Proof.* Let $y = \operatorname{adj}(x) = x^{-1}$. By part 2 of the lemma on elementary properties there is an integer $M \geq 1$ such that $Mx$ and $My$ are integral. Let
$$L = \{ v \in \mathbb{Q}^I : y v \in R_0^I \}.$$
This is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}^I$. It contains $M \mathbb{Z}^I$, since $My$ is integral. It is contained in $M^{-1} \mathbb{Z}^I$: if $yv \in R_0^I$, then $Mv = (Mx)(yv) \in R_0^I \cap \mathbb{Q}^I = \mathbb{Z}^I$ by condition 1. So $L$ is a lattice: a finitely generated subgroup of rank $|I|$. It has a basis. Let $h \in M_I(\mathbb{Q})$ be the matrix whose columns are a basis of $L$, and $g = h^{-1}$. Then $gL = \mathbb{Z}^I$. We show that $k = gx$ and $k^{-1} = yh$ are integral. The columns of $h$ lie in $L$, so $yh$ is integral. Let $x_j$ be the $j$-th column of $x$. By condition 2, $x_j = q + Ma$ with $q \in \mathbb{Q}^I$ and $a \in R_0^I$. Then $yq = e_j - M ya$ is integral, so $q \in L$ and $gq \in \mathbb{Z}^I$. Since $M\mathbb{Z}^I \subseteq L$, the matrix $Mg$ is integral, so $g(Ma) = (Mg)a$ is integral. So $gx_j$ is integral. Now $\det g = \det k$ lies in $R_0 \cap \mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Z}$, and $\det h = \det(yh)$ lies in $\mathbb{Z}$ for the same reason. Their product is $1$, so $\det h = \pm 1$. If $\det h = -1$, then $I$ is not empty. Let $d$ be the diagonal matrix with entries $-1, 1, \dots, 1$, and replace $h$ by $hd$ and $k$ by $dk$. Then $x = hk$ with $\det h = 1$, $\det k = \det x / \det h = 1$, and $k$ integral. $\square$

**Remark 1.1.12 (Formalization).** The fact on polynomials in two matrices of variables in the proof of the lemma on points is `GlobalTheory.T.aeval_sum_elim_eq_zero_of_mem_pts'`; we have not unfolded its proof. The basis of the lattice $L$ comes from `exists_matrix_basis_of_lattice` in [SpecialLinearClassNumberOneAdelic.lean](lean:Development/AbelianVarietyTheory/Proofs/AdelicLatticeProofs/SpecialLinearClassNumberOneAdelic.lean#L83). The class finiteness property is the definition `GlobalTheory.ClassFin.CF`, adelic pairs are `GlobalTheory.ClassFin.AdelicPair`, and rational groups are `GlobalTheory.ClassFin.IsQGroup` over `GlobalTheory.ClassFin.IsPre`. In `CF` only $u$ is asked to be integral, not $u^{-1}$. For a rational group this is the same, since $u^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(u)$ is a polynomial in the entries of $u$ with integer coefficients. The names say "adelic", but every statement of the chapter about $(R, R_0)$ holds for every adelic pair, including $(\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{Z})$.
